Risk-Aware Resource Allocation: What Can Be
Combined from Q and P?

Abstract
Q designs payments that lead strategic agents to a socially preferred
resource allocation, while P gives a distributed method for minimising
conditional value-at-risk from sampled losses. We asked whether P’s risk
estimator could be placed inside Q’s learning scheme to obtain a riskaware mechanism. The comparison found a valid algebraic link, but also
a loss-of-curvature counterexample, a mismatch between the algorithms,
and an information gap for system-wide tail risk. It also separated the
sum of individual risk valuations from the risk of aggregate satisfaction.
The thread ended as a draft because these obstructions are supported,
but the mechanism extension and its learning theorem remain unproved.

1

Introduction

Q studies a resource-allocation problem in which agents have private, random
satisfaction functions and local constraints. Its manager wants to maximise the
sum of their expected satisfactions. Q constructs quadratic payments by a linear matrix inequality so that the unique Nash equilibrium gives the manager’s
allocation, balances the budget, and makes participation worthwhile. It also
gives a decentralised learning method: each agent estimates and solves a proximal best-response problem, then the agents apply a Krasnoselskij update to the
resulting game map [1].
P studies a different distributed problem. Several agents hold copies of one
common decision and cooperatively minimise the average of local conditional
value-at-risk, or CVaR, objectives. They observe sampled losses rather than
gradients. P smooths each CVaR objective, estimates a gradient from sampled
function values, and takes a projected-gradient step after exchanging decisions
with neighbours. With fixed smoothing and finite samples, its agents reach
consensus and approach an empirical, smoothed objective rather than the exact
CVaR objective [2].
The connexion pursued was to make Q risk-aware. For agent n, let xn be its
allocation, let ξn denote its random state, and let ψn (xn , ξn ) be its satisfaction.

What was done

The peers first compared the two algorithms at the level of their actual updates. They found that Q asks each agent to solve a sample-average proximal
best-response problem for its own allocation and price message. Q’s convergence proof assumes a mean-square error bound for that approximate response,
geometrically growing batches, and a nonexpansive exact game map. P instead
takes one projected-gradient step for a common-decision consensus problem. Its
result concerns an empirical, smoothed cooperative objective. This ruled out a
direct insertion of P’s update into Q’s loop.
They next separated the economic mechanism from the learning method. If
tn (s) is agent n’s payment for message profile s, it is fixed once the messages
are fixed. Translation equivariance of CVaR then gives

3

Results

Four conclusions are supported by the record. First, deterministic messagecontingent payments commute with the chosen lower-tail valuation. The displayed translation formula proves that the payment enters risk-adjusted utility
as Rn − tn , just as Q’s payment enters expected utility.
Second, Q’s expectation-level assumptions do not suffice after the substitution. The two-outcome example proves that strong concavity of expected
satisfaction can coexist with a constant lower-tail valuation. The peers’ riskenvelope check supports uniform samplewise strong concavity as a sufficient
repair. Their baseline check likewise supports almost-sure normalisation at zero
as a sufficient condition for Rn (0) = 0.
Third, P supplies estimator-error information but not Q’s missing gamelevel convergence result. Q and P update different objects and rely on different
assumptions. P’s fixed smoothing and finite samples target a surrogate cooperative problem, while Q requires approximate proximal responses for distinct
allocation-price strategies. Exact equilibrium implementation therefore does not
follow from P’s convergence theorem.
Fourth, local marginal information does not determine aggregate CVaR. At
tail level 1/2, the peers compared two equally likely loss couplings. In the first,
the loss vectors are (1, 0) and (0, 1); in the second, they are (1, 1) and (0, 0).
Each agent has the same Bernoulli marginal distribution in both cases, so every
local CVaR observation is the same. Yet the CVaR of total loss is 1 in the first
case and 2 in the second. Local oracles of the kind used by Q and P cannot
therefore evaluate aggregate CVaR without joint-tail information.

4

What did not hold and how objections were
treated

The proposed literal splice did not hold. P’s one-step gradient estimate is not
Q’s approximate proximal best response, and neither paper proves convergence
3

for the combined game. The peers settled this objection by direct comparison
of the update rules and assumptions.
The claim that Q’s economic proof transfers unchanged also did not hold as
stated. CVaR may be nonsmooth, and, more decisively, it may remove strong
concavity altogether. The counterexample settled the claim that Q’s existing primitives are enough. A generalised Karush–Kuhn–Tucker or variationalinequality proof under stronger assumptions remains plausible, but the peers
did not complete it.
Closeness to the correct allocation did not imply budget balance or individual
rationality. Q proves balance at an exact equilibrium using a complementarity
identity, and its payments depend on price messages as well as allocations.
Moreover, Q’s base price domain is unbounded. A bound on payment or budget
error would need a capped or locally bounded price domain and a perturbation
bound for the full message, not merely the allocation. No such bound was
proved.
The two social objectives were not merged. The sum of individual CVaRs
may be the intended welfare measure when individual risk preferences are primary. Aggregate CVaR may instead be intended when the manager cares about
system-wide tail loss. The ledger did not choose between them. The twocoupling example proves that aggregate values are not identifiable from marginal
oracles, but it does not yet produce different optimal allocations. That stronger
impossibility claim remains open.
No outside source or novelty search appears in the ledger. The record therefore supports no novelty claim and gives no broader prior-art comparison.

5

Why the thread stopped

The verifier left the status at DRAFT because the note accurately established
several non-obvious obstructions while keeping conjectures separate from proved
claims. What is missing is a completed positive theorem: either an exact nonsmooth extension of Q for individual CVaR together with a game-specific learning
proof, or an impossibility and information result for aggregate CVaR.
The smallest restart step is to choose the social-risk objective. For the sum
of individual CVaRs, the next step is to prove Q’s mechanism result under
uniform samplewise strong concavity using generalised optimality conditions.
For aggregate CVaR, the next step is to strengthen the marginal-information
example so that two decision-dependent models have the same local oracle laws
but different optimal allocations.
