# A selective opportunity-cost certificate for stalled auctions

## Source interface and sharpened question

Q, Section "Trading Mechanism," executes a buyer's bid when it crosses the
standing ask, at the ask price. Q, Section "Market-Wide Outcomes," measures
allocative efficiency as realized surplus divided by maximum attainable
surplus and attributes much of GPT Large's low trade volume to reluctance
to cross. P, Proposition 1 and Theorem 1, calibrates one episode-level
error radius and uses a margin to certify every non-abstained first-gate
decision in a new episode. P, Sections "Fallback and cost interpretation"
and "Limitations," explicitly withholds any downstream guarantee.

The sharpened question is whether a proposed crossing can be certified
to consume at most a declared amount of *attainable* surplus. This changes
the object of certification from Q's exact exchange predicate to the
opportunity cost of selecting a particular trading pair. The certificate
below says nothing about whether the remaining agents actually trade.

## Why a profitable crossing is insufficient

Consider a remaining two-buyer, two-seller state using reservation values
present in Q: buyer values \(3.25,1.00\) and seller costs \(0.75,3.00\).
The cheap seller posts an ask of \(0.90\). A bid of \(0.90\) from the
\(1.00\)-value buyer crosses profitably and correctly under Q's rules.
That pair creates surplus \(0.25\). The best possible continuation pairs
the \(3.25\)-value buyer with the \(3.00\)-cost seller for another \(0.25\),
giving total surplus \(0.50\). Before the crossing, matching the
\(3.25\)-value buyer to the \(0.75\)-cost seller could attain \(2.50\).
The crossing therefore destroys \(2.00\) of attainable surplus while
remaining individually rational for both parties. Prices divide surplus
but do not alter these amounts.

This is a possible remaining state, not a claim that Q observed this exact
sequence. Q's complete simulator knows all reservation values, so it can
calculate this opportunity cost exactly. A calibrated predictor is useful
only for a deployment observer denied those values, or for a new
experiment with varying and hidden schedules.

## Conditional proposition

Let \(x\) contain the remaining buyers' values and sellers' costs. For
a feasible one-to-one matching \(M\), let

\[
W(M;x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in M}(v_i-c_j),\qquad F(x)=\max_M W(M;x).
\]

The empty matching is allowed. For a candidate edge \(e\), define
\(F_e(x)=\max_{M\ni e}W(M;x)\) and opportunity cost
\(D_e(x)=F(x)-F_e(x)\ge 0\). Suppose a predictor \(\widehat x\), fixed
before this auction begins, obeys
\(\lVert x-\widehat x\rVert_\infty\le\varepsilon\). If no matching has
more than \(m\) edges, each matching's welfare changes by at most
\(2m\varepsilon\). Taking maxima both with and without the edge gives

\[
\bigl|F(x)-F(\widehat x)\bigr|\le 2m\varepsilon,\qquad
\bigl|F_e(x)-F_e(\widehat x)\bigr|\le 2m\varepsilon,
\]

and therefore

\[
D_e(x)\le D_e(\widehat x)+4m\varepsilon.
\]

A selective rule may label the crossing as having opportunity cost at most
\(\tau\) only when \(D_e(\widehat x)+4m\varepsilon\le\tau\); otherwise it
abstains. Individual rationality needs a separate check that the crossing
price lies between the two true reservation values. Q's exchange does
not enforce this constraint, even though its prompt instructs agents to
respect it.

To calibrate \(\varepsilon\), freeze the predictor independently of
calibration, collect exchangeable *market episodes* with complete
reservation vectors, and use
\(R_l=\lVert x_l-\widehat x_l\rVert_\infty\) for each episode. With
\(k=\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil\), take
\(\varepsilon_\alpha=R_{(k)}\), or \(+\infty\) when \(k>n\), exactly as in
P's order-statistic construction. Then the opportunity-cost label is
simultaneously correct for every candidate edge and every remaining
subset in a new episode, with marginal probability at least
\(1-\alpha\). The single vector-error event covers all these
deterministic comparisons, so no per-offer union bound is needed. It
does not give conditional coverage for one market type.

The guarantee requires the predictor to be fixed before the new
market's actions. A predictor updated from the live order history would
need a score covering all possible histories or a separate policy-specific
calibration. Calibrating only on histories generated by Q's original policy
does not establish coverage after an intervention changes future quotes.

## Feasibility test and limitations

Q's published table has only ten-run means, no episode-level predictor
errors. At \(\alpha=0.05\), ten calibration episodes give \(k=11>n\), so
this split-conformal radius is infinite. Q also fixes the reservation
schedule across its reported conditions. A useful test would vary
schedules, hide values from the intervention policy, freeze a predictor
trained on separate runs, collect enough calibration episodes, and report
both coverage and the share of candidate crossings with
\(D_e(\widehat x)+4m\varepsilon\le\tau\). A large radius or near-total
abstention is a negative result.

The proposition certifies *remaining feasible surplus* after one trade.
It does not certify realized terminal welfare, transaction volume,
incentives, price convergence, or a benefit from nudging agents to cross.
An intervention would need a randomized comparison with the unchanged Q
policy, measured at the market-run level. Prior auction literature
already combines conformal prediction and acceptance rules
([Lotan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12016)), so the algebra alone
is not a novelty claim; a new result would need a useful non-vacuous
certificate and measured welfare effect.
