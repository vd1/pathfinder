# Hidden state and delegated reward design

## Question and source claims

The sharpened question is whether Q's robust oversight menu still elicits an unbiased handover approach
when the receiving actor has only an ambiguous view of the held object's orientation. This changes the
broad question of whether the papers have any connection into a test of one construction under a
specified information change.

Q, *Mechanism Design for Alignment and Control*, assumes in its oversight application that the strong
actor observes the state \(\theta\) exactly after the weak monitor chooses an instrument [Q, lines
2017–2022](../inputs/Q.tex). Q's menu \(\mathcal M^*\) uses rewards on \([0,1]\) and isolated extreme
actions outside that interval [Q, lines 2199–2215](../inputs/Q.tex). The proof says a false bias report
sends the actor to an extreme action whenever \(\theta+b-\hat b\) crosses an endpoint [Q, lines
2413–2429](../inputs/Q.tex). Those extreme actions make misreporting costly to the monitor.

P, *When a Donor Wrist Cannot Locate a Rotating Held Object*, gives a conditional matched-observation
limit: distinct object orientations may require different receiving approaches even when the wrist,
encoder, and action histories are indistinguishable [P, lines 43–71](../inputs/P.tex). It does not
establish that such matched histories arise in normal operation or provide their frequency [P, lines
67–71 and 102–108](../inputs/P.tex). P therefore motivates a noisy or coarse actor signal. It does not
provide the signal distribution used below. The DynaMAC and Aero Hand Open papers discussed inside P have
their own local labels; here Q and P denote the assigned files.

## Counterexample to Q's particular menu under coarse observation

Retain Q's quadratic payoffs, state \(\theta\sim U[0,1]\), strong-actor bias \(b\), and weak-monitor bias
\(w\). Change only the actor's state observation to \(s=\mathbf 1\{\theta>1/2\}\). The posterior mean is
\(m_s=\mathbb E[\theta\mid s]\in\{1/4,3/4\}\), and the posterior variance is \(1/48\) for either signal.

For any state-independent reward \(r(a)\), expected actor utility conditional on \(s\) is

\[
\mathbb E[-(a-\theta-b)^2+r(a)\mid s]
=-(a-m_s-b)^2+r(a)-\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid s).
\]

The final term does not affect choice. Thus Q's menu has the same best-response rule as in its proof with
\(\theta\) replaced by \(m_s\). Suppose \(b\) is an interior point of \(\operatorname{supp}(\tau)\),
\(w>0\), and the support permits a report \(\hat b=b-\delta\) where \(0<\delta<\min\{w,1/4\}\). Under a
truthful report the actor chooses \(a=m_s\). Under the false report the actor chooses \(a=m_s+\delta\).
Both choices stay strictly inside \([0,1]\), so neither extreme action is used. The weak monitor's
expected loss changes from

\[
L_W(b)=\mathbb E[(m_s-\theta-w)^2]=1/48+w^2
\]

to

\[
L_W(b-\delta)=\mathbb E[(m_s+\delta-\theta-w)^2]
=1/48+(w-\delta)^2< L_W(b).
\]

The same failure holds for any actor signal whose posterior mean \(M=\mathbb E[\theta\mid s]\) stays
inside \([\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\) almost surely for some \(\varepsilon>0\). If the bias support permits
\(\hat b=b-\delta\) with \(0<\delta<\min\{\varepsilon,2w\}\), a monitor with \(w>0\) induces
\(a=M+\delta\) under Q's unchanged menu, and its loss falls by \(2w\delta-\delta^2>0\). For \(w<0\),
an admissible overreport \(\hat b=b+\delta\) with \(0<\delta<\min\{\varepsilon,-2w\}\) gives the
symmetric improvement. This is a boundary-support condition: Q's extreme-action deterrent cannot fire
for sufficiently small reports when every posterior mean is separated from both endpoints. It does not
show that every imperfect signal defeats the original menu.

For a numerical instance, take \(b=1/2\), \(w=1/10\), \(\delta=1/20\), and any bias support containing
both \(1/2\) and \(9/20\). The monitor's loss falls from \(37/1200\) to \(7/300\), a gain of \(3/400\).
The human's loss rises from \(1/48\) to \(7/300\). The gain and loss follow from the conditional-mean
orthogonality identity and exact fractions.

This is a counterexample to using Q's *unchanged* menu \(\mathcal M^*\) with coarse actor observation. It
is not a counterexample to Q's proposition, whose exact-state observation assumption has been changed. An
alternative menu adapted to the actor's signal may restore truthful bias reporting. The following case
gives such a repair.

There is a simple repair in a structured noisy-signal case, which limits any broader negative claim.
Suppose \(0<p\leq1\) and the actor observes \(s\) generated by a channel that reveals \(\theta\) with
probability \(p\)
and otherwise draws \(s\) independently from \(U[0,1]\). Then \(s\sim U[0,1]\) and its posterior
mean is \(m_s=(1-p)/2+ps\). Set \(\ell=(1-p)/2\), \(x=(a-\ell)/p\), and rescale the two biases as
\(b'=b/p\) and \(w'=w/p\). Conditional expected actor utility, after removing the posterior
variance and dividing by \(p^2\), is exactly Q's quadratic utility with state \(s\sim U[0,1]\).
The monitor's expected loss differs from the transformed Q loss only by the fixed mean posterior
variance. Applying Q's menu to \(x\), with its extreme actions and bias bound scaled in the same way,
therefore elicits \(a=m_s\). Human loss remains the irreducible
\(\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid s)]=(1-p^2)/12\). For \(p<1\), the posterior mean has an
interior support, so the preceding boundary-support argument also shows that Q's unchanged menu fails
whenever the bias support permits a sufficiently small report deviation. The adapted menu succeeds under
the same stipulated channel. This is an affine transfer of Q's proof, not a new
general construction. It shows why the negative result above is specific to leaving the original
menu unchanged when the observation range contracts.

## Information floor and empirical test

Let \(H\) denote every observation available to a receiver before acting, including reports and any fixed
randomization. If no participant in the mechanism has an additional observation correlated with
\(\theta\) conditional on \(H\), every implemented scalar action \(a(H)\) obeys

\[
\mathbb E[(a(H)-\theta)^2]
=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid H)]
+\mathbb E[(a(H)-\mathbb E[\theta\mid H])^2]
\geq\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid H)].
\]

The same statement holds with independent mechanism randomization after taking its expectation. If \(H\)
aliases two equally likely target orientations represented by scalar values separated by \(\Delta\), the
conditional mean-square loss is at least \(\Delta^2/4\). This is a model of a pose-sensitive approach
target, not a measured loss for P's hardware. If a receiver obtains another sensor reading \(Z\), the
best achievable reduction under quadratic loss is

\[
\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid H)]
-\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid H,Z)]
=\mathbb E[(\mathbb E[\theta\mid H,Z]-\mathbb E[\theta\mid H])^2].
\]

This separates what incentives can fix from what sensing can fix in the proposed handover. To determine
practical force, follow P's proposed test [P, lines 92–100](../inputs/P.tex): externally measure the
object-to-wrist transform while the receiver sees matched wrist and motor histories, then test whether
those conditions require incompatible receiving approaches. Estimate the receiver's conditional pose
uncertainty and compare success under wrist-only observation, a new view, and a robust grasp. The
Q-inspired extension would add a monitor that selects reward schedules and measure whether bias-reporting
accuracy changes when the receiver's signal resolution changes. A publication would need such data or a
broader characterization of robust menus under imperfect signals. Neither assigned text supplies it.

## Prior-work check and limits

I searched for `site:arxiv.org mechanism design reward shaping imperfect state information agent robust
first best oversight`, `site:arxiv.org in hand manipulation handover partial observability receiver
object pose information`, and `site:arxiv.org mechanism design delegated rewards private noisy signal
state agent implementability`. These searches are not an exhaustive novelty check. A related
[underactuated-hand pose-estimation study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02843) already investigates haptic
object-pose estimation under occlusion, and P cites other pose-aware handover and contact-tracking work
[P, lines 73–80](../inputs/P.tex). The counterexample and information floor are straightforward; the
publishable question is whether sensor ambiguity materially changes the performance and incentives of
delegated handover control.
