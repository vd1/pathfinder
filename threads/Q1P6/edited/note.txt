Comparison-Based Credit Assignment Through
the Lens of Learning-Augmented Algorithms

Abstract
This note reads a survey of learning-augmented algorithms alongside
MARS-RA, a method that uses multimodal comparisons to assign credit
in multi-agent reinforcement learning. We asked whether comparison error could be related to the performance of the trained agents. We found
that repeated comparisons can reduce sampling error but not semantic
bias, while exact potential-based shaping leaves the underlying objective
unchanged when its rewards are defined consistently. The peers repaired
an ambiguity caused by changing active coalitions, but neither paper supplies the learner-stability result needed for a finite-training guarantee.
The thread therefore ended as a draft with a sound boundary result and
a small correctness repair, but without the larger positive theorem.

Introduction

The survey by Zhao and colleagues, called Q in the research thread, studies
algorithms that use predictions without giving up formal guarantees [2]. It
separates the prediction supplied to an algorithm, the way prediction error is
measured, robustness to arbitrary predictions, and the final task guarantee.
This separation matters when predictions have semantic content or pass through
several stages of a system. A good marginal prediction score does not by itself
give a bound on the system’s final cost.
The paper by Wang and colleagues, called P in the thread, studies credit assignment in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning [1]. Its MARS-RA
method asks a large multimodal model to compare pairs of agents, aggregates
those comparisons with a Bradley–Terry model, and turns the resulting contribution scores into potential-based shaping rewards. The aim is to give agents
useful local feedback when the team reward is delayed and when the active set
of agents changes.
The proposed connexion was that Q’s framework might turn P’s comparison
accuracy into a curve for downstream performance. The thread soon narrowed
this question. Exact potential-based reward shaping does not alter the ordering
of policies under its usual conditions. Comparison quality can therefore affect
the route taken during training, but not the limiting objective. The useful

comparisons improve finite-training performance?

Results

Three conclusions hold on the evidence examined. First, P’s estimator result
concerns concentration around c∗t , the latent Bradley–Terry preference, rather
than around vt∗ , the desired contribution target. Identifying the two is an extra
premise. Ada and Emmy independently checked this distinction against P’s
propositions, and both obtained the sampling-error plus semantic-bias decomposition above.
Second, exact potential-based shaping is prediction-independent at the objective level when its terms are well defined. With a zero terminal potential and
an action-independent initial potential, arbitrary comparison outputs add only
a common offset to discounted return. The identity is pathwise, including for a
realised sequence of biased or stochastic outputs. If one wants a Markov-game
equilibrium statement for history-dependent potentials, the state may need to
include the relevant query history or randomness.

These results also set a limit. Comparison accuracy alone cannot imply a
non-trivial finite-training return bound. Different learners may react differently
to exactly the same shaped signal, and neither Q nor P gives a stability, regret,
or convergence condition that links a reward perturbation to the policy returned
after finite training. The local Lipschitz bound is therefore not an end-to-end
guarantee.

What did not hold

The original plan to transfer Q’s vocabulary directly to P did not hold. P’s
concentration around a modelled preference is not robustness in Q’s sense, which
requires a downstream bound that survives arbitrary predictions. Nor does
pairwise accuracy alone determine which errors occur in frequently visited or
important states. The peers agreed that relabelling P’s result would hide these
distinctions rather than produce a new guarantee.
P’s Bradley–Terry concentration claim was also judged unready for use in
a larger proof. The peers reported inconsistent dependence on population size
between the proposition and appendix, insufficient assumptions for uniform curvature, possible divergence of an unregularised maximum-likelihood estimate
under separated outcomes, and an approximate Taylor step inside the proof.
No corrected rate was derived during the thread. Any query-count trade-off
would first need such a rate under P’s actual sampling and regularisation.
The changing dimension objection was partly settled. P’s fixed population
permits the Rn embedding above, and the all-agent ledger makes the algebra
valid. It did not settle how P’s implementation treats an inactive agent. If
rewards are kept only during an active interval, entry and exit leave boundary terms that can depend on policy. Transition ownership or compensating
transfers would then have to be defined.
A further objection concerned the meaning of the reward. P begins with
a scalar team reward but adds a vector shaping term. The repair can declare
per-agent payoffs and use the componentwise result. Alternatively, it can use
a scalar team potential. Simply summing P’s softmax credits, however, gives a
potential equal to one for every non-empty, non-terminal coalition, so the scalar
signal contains no comparison information. The comparisons can matter only
through redistributed per-agent signals during finite training, which makes the
missing payoff and learner specifications central.
The thread did not assess novelty against work outside Q and P, and the
ledger cited no outside sources. The material is therefore thin beyond the boundary result, the fixed-space repair, and the list of conditions still needed for a
positive theorem.

Why the thread stopped

The final verifier decision was DRAFT. In the verifier’s view, the thread had
established a non-obvious boundary and a valid repair: an all-agent fixedpopulation ledger makes changing-coalition shaping well typed and preserves
payoff differences, while active-only rewards may leave policy-dependent boundary terms. It had also separated sampling error from semantic bias and explained why comparison accuracy cannot by itself guarantee finite-training performance. These points were sound, but they did not amount to the intended
cost-aware finite-training theorem.
The smallest step that would restart the work is to determine whether P’s
implementation records shaping rewards for inactive agents. If it does not, the
next task is to define the smallest entry and exit transfers that reproduce the
full fixed-ledger sum and to check that algebra. A later, larger step would be to
add a valid Bradley–Terry rate and an explicit learner-stability condition before
relating query cost to finite-training return.
