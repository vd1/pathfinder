\section{Introduction}

\paragraph{The pathfinder program.} Pathfinder takes two corpora of scientific
papers (concretely arxiv deposits), $Q$ and $P$, and, using agentic methods, looks for
pairs $(q, p)$ in $Q\times P$ with enough of an interesting connexion to try and
elaborate it as a pre-publication. Ideally, the pre-publication satisfies the
usual constraints of correctness, novelty, relevance, readability, and
exposition of related work in the literature. 

In some cases, the input tuple may be supplemented with a seed note (provided by
the agent that scanned and scored the pair in the first place) that is a
paragraph describing the putative connexion.

This type of task is commonly carried out by human experts. It happens through
seminars that the seed of an idea is provided by someone in the audience, and
such connexions are made, and, in favourable circumstances, further pursued all
the way to a publication. However, by using modern agents (which are competent
experts on every corpus, and always ``willing'' to contribute), one can do it
faster, cheaper, and, therefore, at a larger scale. We have ran it on sets of 
paper of size about a hundred each for a reasonable token budget.

A small epistemologic aside: Pathfinder is about scientific creativity,
(cite Swanson, Cedric Lions, and other refs on creativity by analogy) 
less about closing conjectures, a task at which modern agents have become so good.
Whether this particular attempt at generative creative input is enough or at all capable
of creating new accumulative knowledge remains to be seen.

\paragraph{Object of this note.}
This note purports to describe in implementable terms
a simple agentic process whereby an input tuple
is progressively elaborated and in the favourable cases a pre-publication is
generated. 

As we will see below, the process recruits various agents in various
roles: researchers, verifiers, editors, etc. It consists broadly in two
phases.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The research phase: 
researcher agents collaborate and produce a draft paper via
rounds of interactions with a verifier agent. This is the creative
part of the process which can end on paused thread, or a draft. 
In fact, running this phase again may lead to a different
outcome, possibly a draft expounding on a different idea. 
(The next paragraph gives an example.)
\item The editing phase: 
an author agent produces via rounds of
interactions with a reviewer agent, a final version of the draft.
The final draft can put on pause or accepted as a pre-publication 
by the reviewer.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{An example, and why reruns differ.}
Consider the following $(q,p,c)$ input triplet (where $c$ was written by the scanning agent):

\begin{quote}
$q=\texttt{arXiv:2608.29130}$ ``A Systematic Approach to Mechanism Design with Stochastic Dynamic
Stability'', which designs quadratic payments so
that self-interested agents settle at the welfare-maximising allocation and
learns that equilibrium from sampled feedback

$p=\texttt{arXiv:2609.04460}$ ``Distributed
risk-averse optimization via CVaR'', which minimises
conditional value-at-risk over a network from noisy function values. 

The seed $c$ is: 
replace $q$'s expected-value objectives with
CVaR and use $p$'s gradient estimator inside $q$'s learning loop; does the
payment mechanism still work?
\end{quote}

The thread was run twice on $(q,p,c)$. Both runs quickly rejected the
literal splice ($q$ needs a best response, $p$ supplies a gradient step) and
both ended as \textsc{draft}, but with different results. The first run
proved a positive statement: a uniform bound
\(
\sup_x |\widetilde C_n(x) - C_n(x)| \le L_n\delta_n + (U_n/\alpha_n)(2\pi/s_n)^{1/2}
\)
between $p$'s smoothed, sampled surrogate and the true CVaR objective, and
drew from it welfare and individual-rationality guarantees for the
risk-averse mechanism. The second run proved obstructions instead: a
two-point example in which the expected objective is strongly concave but
its CVaR is constant, so the curvature $q$'s uniqueness proof needs can
vanish under the substitution, and a coupling example showing that the
local risk oracles both papers use cannot determine system-wide tail risk.
Each note is correct, each was accepted by a verifier that had not taken
part.

Thus, the research phase is a sampler. The same input, rerun, may
elicit a different response. Arguably, in the example above, 
a referee would want both responses in one paper.

\paragraph{The various stages of the process}
The process outputs file:
\begin{enumerate}
\item 
a conversation \emph{thread} recording on a ledger of the researcher 
agents chatter
\item a \emph{note} consigning what the researcher agents and verifier 
agreed on (together with a \emph{readable note} of the same, rewritten for a reader 
who never saw the ledger), and,
\item
when accepted by the reviewer, a \emph{paper} 
% (written
% with a literature search and reviewed)
\end{enumerate}
% The outputs above are arranged in increasing order of readability by humans, and 
% decreasing dependence on the history of the generation process.

The readable note presents the research phase’s final outcome for expert assessment,
including unsuccessful or inconclusive outcomes.

\paragraph{Why a state machine.} The work is done by model calls that cost
money, take minutes, often fail or disagree. Writing the pipeline as a machine
with explicit states, transitions and budgets makes it easy to re-implement on
another corpus. It also makes clear the expected behaviours such as:
auditability after the fact (every decision traces to a file and a receipt), and
safe interruptibility: a stop, a crash or an exhausted budget leaves the machine
in a named state from which it resumes, and a human can see at a glance where
each pair stands and what is needed to progress it.

\section{The state of a thread}

One research thread is one instance of the machine; the campaign is a set of instances plus a
scheduler with a purse. Three kinds of actor touch a thread. \emph{Agents} are model calls with a
prompt and a bounded view of the thread; among them two \emph{judges}, the verifier of a note
and the reviewer of a paper, make the only decisions. The \emph{pipeline} is code: it builds,
checks, records and moves the position, and never decides. The \emph{owner} is whoever types in
the terminal, a human or a delegated session; the owner raises budgets, edits by hand and
settles what is paused. Everything an agent sees or writes is a file under the thread's
directory, which is what makes the record auditable after the fact.

\paragraph{Position.} \file{stage}, where the machine is; \file{status}, one of \file{running},
\file{stopped}, \file{BLOCKED} or a terminal word; the counters \file{round} (research rounds
used), \file{repairs} (note repairs used), the paper's \file{round} and the editor's
\file{attempt}.

\paragraph{Memory,} all files under \file{threads/<pair>/}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \file{inputs/}: the two papers, flattened \TeX{} or PDF text, and their metadata; the
    seed $c$, the pair's row of \file{scan.jsonl}, is read from the campaign level.
  \item \file{ledger.jsonl}: append-only attributed entries of kind idea, finding, objection,
    correction, intention, ready or review; the researchers' shared memory and the verifier's
    evidence.
  \item one directory per researcher (\file{ada/}, \file{emmy/}, \ldots): that agent's
    derivations, scripts and checks. The researchers are a campaign setting (\file{peers}); two
    by default, three were tested.
  \item \file{<pair>.tex}: the consolidated note; \file{<pair>.verdict.json}: every verdict with
    the digest of the note it judged.
  \item \file{paper/}: the paper, its bibliography, its search record, the pipeline's checks per
    round, and every review with the digest of the paper it judged.
  \item \file{edited/}: the readable note (\file{note.tex}, \file{references.bib},
    \file{note.pdf}).
  \item \file{status.json}, \file{paper/paper.json}, \file{edited/edit.json}: the position of
    each phase; \file{lock}: the pid of the runner working the thread.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The ledger.} The ledger is the one piece of state several agents write, and its
rules are what make the researchers a group rather than a set of monologues. Entries are
appended through a helper, never edited; each carries a sequence number, an actor, a kind and a
time, and every agent reads the whole ledger before it speaks. A \emph{ready} entry is a claim
about the ledger as it stands: it is void as soon as anyone adds a substantive entry (anything
but ready or review) after it, so a researcher cannot declare itself done and walk away from
an objection. The verifier writes its verdict into the ledger as a review entry, so the next
round's researchers, and the consolidator on a repair, read the judge's reasons where they read
everything else.

\paragraph{Campaign level.} \file{fetch.json} (the two queries and how far each side has been
paged), \file{Q.jsonl} and \file{P.jsonl}, \file{scan.jsonl}, \file{shortlist.json},
\file{receipts.jsonl} (the only spend figure), and the markers \file{stop.json} and
\file{health.json}.

\noindent The research phase takes the incoming message $(q,p,c)$ and ends in one of four
words: \st{draft}, the note carries a result that would justify a paper; \st{pause}, the pair
does not yield anything worth further effort now, or the missing input is one the agents cannot
supply; \st{pause-on-iterate} and \st{pause-on-revise}, the loop named ran out of its budget
with the verifier still asking. Each arrow of the diagram is described below, in the order a
thread meets them.

\paragraph{Admission.} The runner, with no model call, admits a \st{new} pair when a seat is
free and the campaign guard passes: the spend recorded in the receipts plus an estimate for the
calls in flight must stay under \file{budget\_usd}, otherwise the runner writes the stop marker
and admits nothing more. Admission takes the thread's lock, flattens the two papers into
\file{inputs/} and creates one directory per researcher.

\paragraph{Research round.} Every researcher is called concurrently with the prompt
\file{peer.md}, with tools (files, shell, code) and web search. Each sees the two papers, the
seed $c$ with its scores and rationale, the whole ledger, its own directory and its partners'
directories, and the allowance left for this call. It writes ledger entries through the helper
and whatever it likes in its own directory. The first prompt asks the plain question: is there
something interesting to say about this pair, something that could lead to a publication; later
rounds add the verifier's last verdict. A researcher's budget is \file{peer\_calls} calls per
round; the round shares \file{peer\_seconds} of wall time. The round ends when every
researcher's ready stands or the allowance is out. A researcher whose ready still stands when
its turn comes is not called again; it waits for its partners, so a round costs only what the
disagreement costs. If the round ends with an empty ledger the thread ends in \st{pause}.

\paragraph{Consolidate.} The first researcher is called once with \file{consolidate.md} and
tools, sees the ledger, every directory and the prior note if any, and writes the note
\file{<pair>.tex}: what the researchers agreed, with every claim pointing at the ledger entries
that support it and mathematics in \TeX. After an \st{iterate} the note is appended to, so the
history of the thread stays readable; on a \st{revise} the consolidator also sees the
verifier's corrections and rewrites. The call has \file{consolidate\_seconds}; if it produces no
note it is rerun once.

\paragraph{Verify.} A verifier that has taken no part in the thread is called with
\file{verify.md} and no tools; the two papers, the ledger and the note are given inline, so its
only evidence is the record. It writes a verdict file carrying the digest of the note it
judged, a review entry in the ledger, and the position. It answers with one of four words.
\st{draft}: the note contains a supported, non-obvious result about the pair. \st{revise}: the
ledger supports a result but the note misstates it (a dropped result, an overclaim, a missing
attribution), and the consolidator can fix it from the ledger without new research; the
verdict lists the corrections. \st{iterate}: a promising line with a specific gap the
researchers can close with what they have; the verdict names the one thing to do next.
\st{pause}: nothing worth further effort now, or a gap that needs data, experiments or access
the agents do not have; the verdict says what would be needed so that a human can decide. The
verifier is told not to ask twice: if a previous \st{iterate} asked for something the ledger
then shows the researchers could not supply, it must answer \st{pause} and name the missing
input. The call has \file{verify\_seconds}; an empty reply is retried once; an unreadable
verdict is saved beside the position and the thread is \file{BLOCKED}.

\paragraph{The iterate loop.} On \st{iterate} the round counter goes up and the researchers
are called again, with the verdict in the ledger. The cap is \file{rounds}, four in the
reference campaign; at the cap the thread ends in \st{pause-on-iterate}.

\paragraph{The revise loop.} On \st{revise} the repair counter goes up and the consolidator is
called again with the corrections. The cap is \file{repairs}, one; at the cap the thread ends
in \st{pause-on-revise}. A repair is a rewrite of the note from the same ledger; it costs one
call, which is why it has its own word and its own cap rather than spending a research round.

\noindent The edit phase has two independent branches. The upper one, the \emph{paper}, runs
only on a \st{draft}: an author agent writes a paper from the note and a reviewer judges it,
and the loop ends \st{accepted} or \st{pause-on-amend}. The lower one, the \emph{readable
note}, runs once for every thread whatever its ending, so that even a paused pair leaves an
account a colleague can read without the ledger. Neither branch changes the note or the
ledger; the research record is frozen when the research phase ends.

\paragraph{Author.} The author agent is called with \file{author.md}, tools and web search, in
the thread's directory, so it sees everything the researchers left: the two papers, the ledger,
the note, the verdicts, every directory. It also sees the previous review's findings, if this
is a later round. It writes \file{paper/paper.tex}, \file{paper/references.bib} and
\file{paper/search.md}, the record of the literature searches it ran and what they returned. It
is asked for a short paper in the usual shape (abstract, introduction, what the two papers
establish, the result, what did not hold, related work, limitations), with every claim traced
to the note or the ledger and every reference verified against arXiv or a DOI. The call has
\file{paper\_seconds}.

\paragraph{Build and check.} Before any reviewer sees the paper the pipeline, with no model,
builds it with \file{latexmk} and checks the bibliography: every cited key exists, every entry
is cited, every entry carries a URL, DOI or arXiv identifier, and every arXiv identifier's title
matches the arXiv record. The findings are written to \file{paper/checks-round-N.txt} and shown
to the reviewer; a failed build is a finding too.

\paragraph{Review.} A reviewer that has taken no part is called with \file{review.md} and no
tools; it is given inline the paper, the bibliography, the pipeline's checks, the author's
search record, the note, the ledger and the two papers. It reads the paper as an editor would
before sending it to referees and checks that every claim traces to the note or ledger without
strengthening it, that the mathematics is correct and the notation defined, that $q$ and $p$
are represented fairly and what is new is separated from what they establish, that no reference
looks invented, and that the related-work claim is supported by the recorded searches. It
answers \st{accept} or \st{amend} with findings by identifier, each marked major or minor.
\st{accept} may carry minor findings, the kind an author applies while formatting; \st{amend}
is reserved for a finding that changes a claim, its scope, its attribution or its correctness,
or that a referee would hold against the paper. A reviewer asked for a paper with no findings at
all never converges, because every revision opens new minor points; this rule is what lets the
loop end. The review is recorded with the digest of the paper it judged. The call has
\file{review\_seconds}; the loop's cap is \file{paper\_rounds}, three; at the cap the paper
ends \st{pause-on-amend}.

\paragraph{The amend loop.} On \st{amend} with rounds left, the author is called again with
the findings and must say what it did about each. The pipeline builds and checks again, and the
reviewer reads the new version.

\paragraph{The owner's moves.} A paper at \st{pause-on-amend} has two ways forward, both the
owner's. The owner can raise \file{paper\_rounds} and rerun the paper command, which resumes
the loop where it stopped. Or the owner can edit the paper by hand and ask for one reviewer
round with \file{paper PAIR --review}: no author call, the paper as it stands, counted as the
next round and outside the budget. In the reference campaign one paper went through three such
rounds; the reviewer caught a real error in the owner's own edit before accepting.

\paragraph{Editor.} For every thread with an ending, the editor agent is called with
\file{editor.md} and tools but no web search. It sees the two papers and their metadata, the
note, the verdicts, the ledger and every directory, and writes \file{edited/note.tex} with a
bibliography: the note rewritten as a short readable paper, in plain terms, for a reader who
never saw the ledger, keeping the structure of a paper (what was asked, what was done, what
holds, what did not hold, why the thread stopped and what would restart it). The pipeline
builds it and runs the same reference checks as for the paper, and refuses the result if a
cited key is missing, an arXiv title does not match, or an identifier is unknown; on a refusal
the editor is called once more with the findings and told to fix them in place. Two failed
attempts end in \file{blocked}. The call has \file{edit\_seconds}.

\paragraph{Words and endings.} The two judges' words, and where each phase can end:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
phase & decisions of its judge & endings \\
\midrule
research & \st{draft}, \st{revise}, \st{iterate}, \st{pause} & \st{draft}, \st{pause}, \st{pause-on-iterate}, \st{pause-on-revise} \\
paper & \st{accept}, \st{amend} & \st{accepted}, \st{pause-on-amend}, \file{blocked} \\
readable note & none & \st{done}, \file{blocked} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Every \st{pause-on-x} means ``the loop named X ran out of its budget with the judge still
asking''; the owner can raise the budget and resume from the recorded stage. \file{stopped} and
\file{BLOCKED} are not endings. \file{stopped} is a drain checkpoint (a stop marker, a transport
failure, a kill) and resumes at the recorded stage on the next start. \file{BLOCKED} is an
unreadable decision or a missing output: the reply is saved beside the position, the runner
never re-admits the thread, and \file{reconcile} names the one safe action.

Between transitions the pipeline itself, with no model, does the checks that gate the next
state: it builds the \LaTeX, verifies the references, records digests, and writes the position.
The verifier's and reviewer's judgements are the only decisions; every other arrow is mechanical
or the owner's.

\end{center}

\noindent Every model call appends a receipt, so the campaign budget is checked against the sum
of receipts, never against an estimate alone. A call that times out with no usage is charged at
\file{call\_estimate\_usd}. Owner rounds are not budgeted: the owner is the budget.

\section{Interrupts and hazards}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item A stop marker (operator, guard, or first Ctrl-C) stops admission; calls in flight land
    and write their checkpoints; the runner exits; the next start resumes each pair at its
    recorded stage.
  \item No session within the grace time is a transport failure: no receipt, the pair is
    \file{stopped}, two in a row set the health marker and pause admission until a probe
    succeeds. The \file{paper} and \file{edit} commands skip the failed pair and continue.
  \item \file{reconcile} reads the position of one pair and names the one safe action: start,
    resume peers, run consolidate, run verify, or nothing; \file{--apply} performs it.
  \item The lock protects a thread from two runners. It does not protect the paper or editor
    stage from two concurrent commands on the same pair; that is an operator error the code
    does not yet catch.
\end{itemize}

\appendix
\section{Agent resources against human resources, per pair}
\label{app:resources}

\paragraph{The campaign.} The reference campaign ran on September 11-13 2026.
The two corpora were the ten most recent arXiv papers for the queries
``mechanism design'' ($Q$, dated 28 August to 4 September) and ``agentic
cooperation'' ($P$, 16 July to 3 September). A Claude Sonnet judge scanned all
100 pairs from titles and abstracts, giving each a feasibility and a gain on a
scale of 0 to 100 and a one-paragraph connexion that serves as a seed; the score
is their product, and the 14 pairs at or above 1500 were selected. Each thread
ran two Opus 5 researcher agents with web search. The caps were \file{rounds} 4,
\file{repairs} 1 and \file{paper\_rounds} 3, under a campaign budget of 200~USD.
Outcomes: 5 threads ended \textsc{draft}, 8 \textsc{pause} and 1
\textsc{pause-on-iterate}; the 5 drafts became papers, 4 accepted by the
reviewer within the run and 1 after the owner's edits; all 14 threads got a
readable note. Total: 330 model calls and 187~USD at API prices.

Agent figures are taken from the receipts of that campaign, as medians
over threads. Means are dragged up by three threads that hit time-outs and
by one paper whose seven review rounds were spread over two days. Human
figures are estimates for a postdoc-level researcher at a fully loaded cost
of about 60~USD per hour, stated so that they can be rescaled.

Per pair the agent pipeline is therefore roughly 500 times cheaper and 100
times faster on the calendar. At the scale of a campaign the gap is what
makes the method exist at all: scanning 100 pairs costs 3~USD against 25
human hours, and the 14 threads and 5 papers of the reference campaign cost
175~USD, about three loaded postdoc days, against something like a working
year.

Three caveats keep the comparison fair.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \emph{Not the same quality.} The accepted papers are correct
    technical remarks, the kind a good student writes in a week, not the
    pair a senior researcher would choose nor the paper they would write.
    A human would also never look at 100 pairs; taste replaces breadth.
  \item \emph{Human time does not go to zero.} The owner still reads the
    readable notes, clears the decision queue and settles the paused
    pairs: on this campaign of the order of 10 to 20 minutes per pair, plus
    an hour of attention and about 12~USD for the one paper that was
    rescued by hand. The pipeline moves human effort from producing to
    choosing and reading.
  \item \emph{Wall time is set by the slowest call, not by the sum.} Peers
    run concurrently, so a thread's clock is its longest call and its
    time-outs; the 8-minute median hides a 6-hour worst case.
\end{itemize}
